1. End of Cash Sorting and the Sweep Deposit Turn
Client cash sorting — the 2022-2023 exodus of sweep balances into higher-yielding alternatives — has reversed into sustained sweep deposit growth, but the pace and durability of that reversal need scrutiny rather than a one-line "it's fixed" claim.
[a] Establish the arc: describe the 2022-2023 cash-sorting drag (clients reallocating out of sweep into purchased money market funds/fixed income, forcing reliance on higher-cost funding) versus the 2025 reversal, where bank sweep deposits and payables to brokerage clients rose by a combined $36.6 billion (12%) for the full year, evidencing decelerating outflows turning into net inflows.
[b] Note the structural assist from the 2023 IDA agreement amendment: after September 10, 2025 Schwab gained broader discretion to pull balances back from the TD Depository Institutions and transferred $6.7 billion of bank deposit account (BDA) balances onto its own balance sheet, converting fee-based off-balance-sheet cash into on-balance-sheet funding for interest-earning assets.
[c] Test durability using the most recent quarter: client transactional sweep cash ended March 2026 at $461.5 billion, up $7.8 billion versus year-end 2025 — assess whether this quarterly pace is consistent with, decelerating from, or reaccelerating versus the roughly $9 billion average quarterly gain implied by the 2025 full-year increase, rather than assuming the recovery is now on autopilot.

2. Net Interest Margin Recovery: Mechanics, Not Magic
Net interest margin expansion from 2.12% (2024) to 2.74% (2025) to 2.88% (1Q26) is a real and large move, but it is a composite of several offsetting forces that an analyst must unpack rather than treat as a single undifferentiated tailwind.
[a] Decompose the stated NIM/net interest revenue drivers across periods: net interest revenue rose 28% to $11.8 billion in 2025 (from $9.1 billion in 2024; $11,750 million vs $9,144 million per the detailed tables) primarily on lower bank supplemental funding balances, lower average rates paid on funding sources, and growth in margin/bank lending — partially offset by lower yields on floating-rate assets; in 1Q26 net interest revenue grew 16% year-over-year to $3,144 million ($3.1 billion) with NIM at 2.88% versus 2.53% in 1Q25, driven by the same funding-cost/lending-growth mix.
[b] Flag the deceleration: net interest revenue growth slowed from 28% (FY2025 vs FY2024) to 16% (1Q26 vs 1Q25) even as NIM kept expanding, implying the easiest gains from unwinding high-cost funding are being progressively exhausted.
[c] Weigh the risk-factor counter-evidence: the 10-K explicitly warns that declining short-term rates can compress yields on floating-rate assets and reduce net interest revenue/bank deposit account fee revenue faster than funding costs fall, meaning the same rate-cutting cycle that lowered supplemental funding costs is also a headwind to asset yields.
[d] Optionally benchmark against peers to test whether the NIM recovery is Schwab-specific or a shared industry tailwind: IBKR's net interest income rose from $770 million (1Q25) to $904 million (1Q26, +17%) and Morgan Stanley's rose from $2,353 million (1Q25) to $2,703 million (1Q26, +15%) — comparable growth rates to Schwab's 16% suggest part of the recovery reflects a broader funding-cost cycle, not purely Schwab's idiosyncratic cash-sorting unwind.

3. Paydown of High-Cost Supplemental Funding as the Reversal Engine — and Its Limits
The unwind of bank supplemental funding (FHLB borrowings, brokered CDs, repo) has been the single largest lever behind the margin recovery, but that lever is now largely spent, which changes what can drive further NIM gains.
[a] Quantify the paydown trajectory: bank supplemental funding fell from a May 2023 peak, to $49.9 billion at year-end 2024 (down 37% for the year), to $5.1 billion at year-end 2025 (down $44.8 billion, or 90%, in 2025 alone), with FHLB borrowings at zero and only $2.4 billion of brokered CDs (weighted-average rate 3.77%) remaining as of March 31, 2026.
[b] Identify how the paydown was funded internally — principal and interest runoff from the AFS/HTM securities portfolios, the BDA balance transfers, and normal client cash inflows — rather than external capital raises, which is why it dropped straight to net interest revenue instead of diluting shareholders.
[c] Synthesize the limits: with supplemental funding balances now near-fully wound down (~$5 billion or less versus a ~$50-80 billion base two years ago), reconcile why the mechanical "cheap-funding-replaces-expensive-funding" trade that drove most of the 2024-2025 NIM expansion has little room left to run, and what that implies for whether further NIM gains must instead come from continued margin/bank-loan growth or from the pace of Fed rate cuts — do not simply restate the paydown figures as if they guarantee further margin expansion.

4. Organic Growth and Capital Return as the Offset — and the Balance-Sheet Trade-off
Schwab is simultaneously funding balance-sheet growth (margin lending) and returning capital aggressively even as its GAAP leverage ratio has been falling, a tension that requires explicit reconciliation rather than parallel reporting of "growth is strong" and "capital return is strong."
[a] Establish organic growth momentum as a complement to the NIM recovery: core net new assets totaled $519.4 billion in 2025 (+42% year-over-year, a 5.1% annualized organic growth rate) and $140.0 billion in 1Q26 (+2% year-over-year, including a $17.5 billion planned mutual fund clearing client deconversion; $157.5 billion excluding that item), while total client assets reached $11.90 trillion at year-end 2025 before dipping 1% to $11.77 trillion by March 2026 on equity-market declines.
[b] Note margin lending as a second, cash-sorting-independent growth engine: margin loans grew to $112.3 billion at year-end 2025 (+34%) and $126.7 billion by March 2026 (+13% quarter-over-quarter, +52% year-over-year), funded in part by wholesale borrowing — meaning fast-growing margin lending could reintroduce reliance on the same higher-cost funding sources the company just spent two years paying down if sweep deposit growth doesn't keep pace.
[c] Synthesize the capital-return tension explicitly: reconcile how Schwab returned $11.8 billion of total capital in 2025 (including $7.3 billion of buybacks and an 8% dividend increase, plus a $2.5 billion Series G preferred redemption) and a further $2.4 billion of 1Q26 buybacks alongside a 19% dividend hike, even as its consolidated (GAAP) Tier 1 Leverage Ratio fell from 9.9% (year-end 2024) to 9.3% (year-end 2025) to 8.9% (1Q26) — while the adjusted (AOCI-inclusive) Tier 1 Leverage Ratio actually rose to 7.1% at year-end 2025 from 6.8% — and take a clear position on whether the pace of capital return is sustainable given continued margin-loan-driven balance-sheet growth, or whether it is quietly re-levering the company just as the cash-sorting/funding recovery is running out of room.
